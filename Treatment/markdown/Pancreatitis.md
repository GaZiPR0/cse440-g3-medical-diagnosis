# 🫁 Pancreatitis

> **In short:** Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas — the gland that makes digestive enzymes and blood-sugar hormones. It can flare up suddenly (acute) or persist for years (chronic).

| | |
|---|---|
| **Affected organ** | Pancreas |
| **Two forms** | Acute and chronic |
| **Common causes** | Gallstones, alcohol |
| **Severity** | Mild to life-threatening |

---

## Overview

**Pancreatitis** is inflammation of the pancreas — a long, flat gland tucked behind the stomach in the upper abdomen. The pancreas produces enzymes that help digestion, along with hormones (such as insulin) that regulate how the body handles sugar.

- **Acute pancreatitis** — appears suddenly and lasts for days. Often mild and resolves with care, but can occasionally become severe.
- **Chronic pancreatitis** — develops and persists over many years, gradually damaging the gland.

Mild cases may clear without treatment, but severe cases can cause life-threatening complications.

## Symptoms

- Upper abdominal pain, often spreading to the back
- Nausea and vomiting
- Fever
- Rapid pulse
- A tender abdomen

The pain may worsen after eating. Chronic pancreatitis can also cause weight loss and oily, foul-smelling stools as digestion is affected.

## Diagnosis

- **Blood tests** — look for raised levels of pancreatic enzymes.
- **Stool tests** — in chronic cases, measure fat levels that suggest poor nutrient absorption.
- **CT scan** — checks for gallstones and the extent of inflammation.
- **Abdominal ultrasound** — looks for gallstones and pancreas inflammation.
- **Endoscopic ultrasound** — checks for inflammation or blockages in the pancreatic or bile duct.
- **MRI** — looks for abnormalities in the gallbladder, pancreas, and ducts.

## Treatment

**Mild acute pancreatitis** — Care supports the body while the pancreas heals itself:

- **Painkillers** to control discomfort.
- **Bowel rest** — no food or drink by mouth for a few days until things improve.
- **IV fluids** to prevent dehydration (usually for the first 24–48 hours).
- A **nasogastric tube** may be used to ease nausea and vomiting.

Most people can go home after about 5–7 days.

**Severe acute pancreatitis** — Severe cases can involve tissue death, raising the risk of sepsis and hypovolemic shock — both life-threatening. Treatment is given in intensive care and may include antibiotics, IV fluids, breathing support, feeding tubes, and sometimes surgery to remove dead tissue.

**Treating the cause** — If **gallstones** caused it, surgery (or an ERCP procedure) to remove them and the gallbladder may follow once the person recovers. If **alcohol misuse** was the cause, a treatment program may be offered.

## Prevention & Self-Care

- Limit or avoid alcohol.
- Don't smoke.
- Eat a low-fat, balanced diet and stay hydrated.
- Maintain a healthy weight to lower the risk of gallstones.
