# 👁️ Dry Eye Syndrome

> **In short:** Eyes that do not stay properly wet, either from too few tears or tears that evaporate too fast. They feel gritty and tired, and can water paradoxically.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Affects** | The tear film |
| **Key sign** | Gritty, sandy feeling |
| **Big trigger** | Screen use |
| **Mainstay** | Lubricating eye drops |

---

## Overview

A healthy eye is covered by a thin **tear film** with three layers — an oily outer layer that stops evaporation, a watery middle layer, and an inner layer that helps it stick. Every blink spreads it fresh across the surface.

**Dry eye syndrome** occurs when that film fails: either not enough tears are made, or they evaporate too quickly because the oil glands in the eyelids are blocked. The surface is left exposed and irritated. It is one of the most common eye complaints, and it has become far more common with long hours in front of screens — people blink around half as often when concentrating on a display.

Age, contact lenses, air conditioning, fans, dusty air, certain medicines (antihistamines, some blood-pressure and antidepressant tablets) and conditions such as Sjögren's syndrome all contribute.

## Symptoms

- A gritty or sandy feeling, as though something is in the eye
- Dry, tired eyes after reading or screen work
- Redness of the eyes
- Blurred vision that clears when you blink
- Sensitivity to light, wind and air conditioning
- A burning or stinging sensation
- Stringy mucus around the eye
- Difficulty wearing contact lenses

> 💡 **Why do dry eyes water?** it sounds contradictory, but a dry, irritated surface triggers a reflex flood of watery tears. These lack the oily layer, so they run down the cheek instead of coating the eye — and the dryness continues. Watering eyes are a classic sign of dry eye.

> ⚠️ **See an eye doctor if:** you have eye pain rather than discomfort, sudden changes in vision, marked light sensitivity, discharge or pus, or symptoms that do not improve with drops. Left untreated, severe dry eye can damage the surface of the cornea.

## Diagnosis

An eye doctor examines the eyelids and the surface of the eye with a **slit lamp**. A **Schirmer test** uses a small strip of paper tucked under the lower lid to measure how much tear is produced. A dye such as fluorescein shows up dry patches and how quickly the tear film breaks up, and the oil glands along the lid margin are checked for blockage.

## Treatment

Dry eye is usually a long-term condition that is managed rather than cured — but it is managed well, and most people become comfortable.

### Everyday relief

- **Lubricating eye drops (artificial tears)** — the mainstay; use preservative-free drops if you need them more than four times a day
- Thicker gels or ointments at bedtime for overnight relief
- **Warm compresses** on closed lids for five minutes, then a gentle lid massage, to unblock the oil glands
- Clean the lid margins gently with a clean cloth or a lid-cleaning solution

### The 20-20-20 rule

Every **20 minutes**, look at something about **20 feet** away for **20 seconds**, and blink fully several times. This one habit does more for screen-related dry eye than almost anything else.

### When drops are not enough

- Prescription anti-inflammatory drops such as ciclosporin
- Short courses of steroid drops under an eye doctor's supervision
- **Punctal plugs** — tiny plugs in the tear ducts that keep your own tears on the eye for longer
- Treating the underlying cause, such as blepharitis or a medicine you are taking

## Prevention

- Position screens slightly below eye level so the lids cover more of the eye
- Keep fans and air-conditioning vents from blowing directly at your face
- Use a humidifier in dry or heavily air-conditioned rooms
- Wear wrap-around sunglasses outdoors in wind and dust
- Give contact lenses a break, and never sleep in them unless prescribed for that
- Drink enough water and get adequate sleep
- Avoid smoke, including your own
