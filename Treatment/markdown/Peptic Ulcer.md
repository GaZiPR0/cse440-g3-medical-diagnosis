# 🔥 Peptic Ulcers

> **In short:** Peptic ulcers are open sores that form on the lining of the stomach or the upper small intestine. The most common cause is H. pylori bacteria or long-term use of NSAID painkillers.

## Overview

**Peptic ulcers** are open sores that develop on the inside lining of your stomach and the upper part of your small intestine. The most common symptom is stomach pain. There are two main kinds:

- **Gastric ulcers** — occur on the inside of the stomach.
- **Duodenal ulcers** — occur on the inside of the upper small intestine (the duodenum).

The most common causes are infection with **H. pylori** bacteria and long-term use of aspirin and other NSAIDs. Stress and spicy food don't cause ulcers, but they can make symptoms worse.

## Symptoms

- Burning stomach pain
- Bloating
- Feeling full quickly
- Nausea
- Heartburn

> ⚠️ **Warning signs — see a doctor promptly:** vomiting blood or dark material, black or tarry stools, sudden severe stomach pain, or unexplained weight loss can signal bleeding or a serious complication.

## Diagnosis

- **Tests for H. pylori** — a breath test is most accurate; stool tests are also used. (Blood tests are generally unreliable.) Tell your doctor if you're taking antacids, as they can cause false-negative results.
- **Endoscopy** — a thin tube with a lens is passed down the throat to look for ulcers, and a biopsy may be taken. This is more likely if you're older or have signs of bleeding or weight loss. A stomach ulcer usually gets a follow-up endoscopy after treatment to confirm healing.
- **Upper GI series (barium swallow)** — X-rays taken after swallowing a barium liquid that coats the digestive tract and makes an ulcer more visible.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause.

- **If H. pylori is present** — a combination of antibiotics (to clear the infection) plus a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) to reduce stomach acid, usually taken for up to two weeks. Mild side effects such as diarrhoea are possible.
- **If there's no H. pylori** — a prescription or over-the-counter PPI (such as omeprazole or lansoprazole) for up to eight weeks to lower acid and let the ulcer heal.
- **Acid blockers** such as famotidine can also reduce acid and ulcer pain.
- **Sucralfate** coats the stomach lining and eases symptoms.

## Prevention

- Avoid frequent or high-dose use of NSAID painkillers; ask about safer alternatives.
- Wash hands and eat properly cooked food to reduce H. pylori risk.
- Limit alcohol and don't smoke.
