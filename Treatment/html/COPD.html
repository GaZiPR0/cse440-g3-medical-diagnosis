🔎 Overview

COPD is a long-term lung condition in which the airways are narrowed and the air sacs damaged, so air cannot flow out of the lungs freely. It covers two overlapping problems: chronic bronchitis, where the airways are inflamed and produce excess mucus, and emphysema, where the walls of the air sacs are destroyed.

The leading cause is smoking, but long exposure to cooking smoke, dust and air pollution matters greatly too, and COPD is common in people who have never smoked a cigarette. The damage builds slowly over years, and by the time breathlessness is noticed a good deal of lung function has already been lost. It cannot be undone — but its progress can be slowed dramatically, above all by stopping exposure to smoke.

🩺 Symptoms

  • Breathlessness during everyday activities such as walking or climbing stairs
  • A cough that brings up mucus on most days for months at a time
  • Wheezing or a whistling sound in the chest
  • Tightness in the chest
  • Frequent chest infections that take a long time to clear
  • Tiredness, and unintended weight loss in later stages
  • Bluish lips or fingernails when oxygen levels fall
COPD or asthma?asthma symptoms come and go, often start young, and respond well to inhalers. COPD is persistent, usually appears after the age of 40, is strongly linked to smoke exposure, and gets slowly worse over years.
Seek urgent help if:you are severely breathless at rest or cannot speak in full sentences, your lips or fingers turn blue, you have chest pain, a high fever, or you feel confused or unusually drowsy. This may be a flare-up needing immediate treatment.

🧪 Diagnosis

COPD is confirmed with spirometry, a simple breathing test in which you blow as hard and as long as you can into a machine. It measures how much air you can push out in one second, and whether that obstruction persists after an inhaler — which is what separates COPD from asthma.

A chest X-ray or CT scan, a blood oxygen reading, and blood tests may be added to rule out other causes and judge severity.

💊 Treatment

Stopping smoke exposure comes first

Nothing else changes the course of COPD as much. Quitting smoking — and moving away from smoky cooking fires and polluted air — will not restore lost lung tissue, but it slows further decline more effectively than any medicine. Ask your doctor about support and nicotine replacement; most people need more than one attempt, and that is normal.

Medicines

  • Bronchodilator inhalers to relax and open the airways — the mainstay of daily treatment
  • Steroid inhalers, usually combined with a bronchodilator, for frequent flare-ups
  • Antibiotics and steroid tablets during a flare-up, when a doctor prescribes them
  • Oxygen therapy at home for people whose blood oxygen stays low

Beyond medicines

  • Pulmonary rehabilitation — a supervised exercise and education programme that reliably improves breathlessness and quality of life
  • Yearly influenza vaccination and the pneumococcal vaccine
  • Learning and using correct inhaler technique — poor technique is a very common reason treatment seems not to work
  • Breathing techniques such as pursed-lip breathing
  • Good nutrition, since breathing itself burns energy

🛡️ Prevention

  • Do not smoke, and avoid other people's smoke
  • Use good ventilation or a cleaner stove when cooking with wood, coal or biomass
  • Wear proper protection if you work with dust, fumes or chemicals
  • Keep vaccinations up to date to prevent chest infections
  • See a doctor early about a cough or breathlessness that will not go away
